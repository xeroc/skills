**Version:** 1.0
**Purpose:** Use the Pantalon method (Yale-based influence research) to move anyone from mild interest to personal conviction by making _them_ articulate the reasons, the readiness, the outcome, and the next step. It works for sales, pitches, negotiations, storytelling, persuasion, alignment, and any moment where someone needs to convince themselves.

---

## Core Principle

> **Never lead with your reasons. Ask for theirs.**

The moment you list benefits, the other person's brain shifts to counter-arguing. The moment _they_ list benefits, they start selling themselves. People believe their own reasons, not yours.

---

## The Skill in One Line

**Ask. Rate. Reverse. Imagine. Deepen. Shrink.**

---

## The Six Moves

### 1. The Flip — Ask for Their Reasons

**Question:** _"Why might you want to fix this?"_

Don't pitch. Don't list benefits. Ask what's pulling them toward a change. The second they answer, they've started building their own case.

**Use when:** Opening any conversation where you need buy-in.
**Avoid:** Leading with your solution, your features, your story.

---

### 2. The Number — Force a Rating

**Question:** _"On a scale of 1 to 10, how ready are you?"_

Normal questions get autopilot answers. An odd, specific question makes them stop and think. When they say a number out loud, they've committed to a real position.

**Use when:** You need to gauge genuine interest without asking "are you interested?"
**Why it works:** The number pulls the truth out. Vague enthusiasm becomes a concrete data point.

---

### 3. The Reverse — Ask Why Not Lower

**Question:** _"Why didn't you pick a lower number?"_

This is the most powerful move. It feels backwards. It is.

- Ask "why not higher?" → they defend the opposite. They list reasons they're _not_ ready.
- Ask "why not lower?" → they list reasons they _are_ ready. They talk themselves into their own interest.

**Use when:** They've given you a number (or any signal of interest) and you want to strengthen it.
**Never:** Ask why not higher.

**Storytelling version:** _"Why didn't you walk away earlier?"_ → they explain their own commitment.

---

### 4. The After — Let Them Build the Picture

**Question:** _"Imagine it's handled. What's different for you?"_

Don't paint the picture. Let them paint it. When the buyer describes the win in their own words, it stops being your pitch and becomes their idea.

**Use when:** Moving from problem to solution, or from interest to desire.
**Storytelling version:** _"Imagine this ended well. What does that look like?"_

---

### 5. The Deepener — Follow Every Reason with "Why does that matter?"

**Question:** _"Why does that matter to you?"_

Every time they give a reason, ask why it matters. They go one layer deeper into their own motivation. Two or three layers in, mild interest becomes something personal.

**Use when:** They've given you a surface reason. Keep going.
**Storytelling version:** _"And why does that matter to the character?"_ → reveals theme.

---

### 6. The Shrink — End on the Smallest Safe Step

**Question:** _"What's one small step you could take?"_

Not "are you ready to sign." Not "will you commit." The smallest safe move. A giant ask triggers the flinch. A tiny one gets a yes. And the first small yes is the hardest one to walk back.

**Use when:** Closing anything — a sale, a decision, a behavior change, a story beat.
**Storytelling version:** End on the smallest irreversible step the character takes, not the biggest.

---

## The Full Sequence (Anytime You Need to Convince)

| Step | Ask                                       | What It Does                    |
| ---- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| 1    | "Why might you want to fix this?"         | They state their own reasons    |
| 2    | "On a scale of 1–10, how ready are you?"  | They commit to a number         |
| 3    | "Why didn't you pick a lower number?"     | They argue _for_ their interest |
| 4    | "Imagine it's handled. What's different?" | They build the after            |
| 5    | "Why does that matter to you?" (×2–3)     | They go deeper into motivation  |
| 6    | "What's one small step you could take?"   | They commit to a tiny yes       |

**Result:** They've talked themselves from mild interest to personal conviction — in their own words, on their own terms.

---

## Applying It to Storytelling

The same six moves map onto narrative:

1. **Flip** — Don't tell the audience why the character should change. Show the character asking what's wrong.
2. **Number** — Give the character a concrete measure of their own readiness (a countdown, a test, a threshold).
3. **Reverse** — Let the character ask "why didn't I quit earlier?" — they explain their own commitment.
4. **After** — Let the character imagine the resolved state. Don't narrate it for them.
5. **Deepen** — Every motive gets a "why does that matter?" — that's theme.
6. **Shrink** — End on the smallest step, not the biggest. The tiny yes is the hardest to walk back.

---

## Rules of the Skill

- **Never lead with your reasons.** Ask for theirs first.
- **Never ask "why not higher."** Always ask "why not lower."
- **Never describe the after for them.** Let them describe it.
- **Never end on the biggest ask.** End on the smallest safe step.
- **Never accept the first reason.** Follow it with "why does that matter?"
- **Never skip the number.** The rating pulls the truth out.

---

## One-Sentence Summary

**Ask for their reasons, make them rate it, reverse the scale, let them build the after, deepen every motive, and close on the smallest step — and they'll convince themselves.**
