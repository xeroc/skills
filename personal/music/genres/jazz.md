Great topic — jazz cadenzas (the free, out-of-time passages usually right before the final tonic) are where a lot of the emotional weight of a performance lives. Here's a real, practical breakdown of what makes them work.

## What a cadenza actually is

It's usually the moment right before the last chord of a tune — most often at the end of a ballad — where the time stops, the rhythm section either drops out or holds a fermata, and the soloist (or comping instrument) plays freely, without a fixed pulse, before resolving to the tonic. Think of how Bill Evans or Keith Jarrett close out a ballad, or how a horn player takes a long unaccompanied run before the last note.

## The emotional arc — structure first

A cadenza that "sounds relieving" needs shape, not just pretty notes. A reliable arc:

1. **Depart from the pulse** — slow down or completely release time right as the tune winds down.
2. **Wander through tension** — explore harmony away from "home," using chromaticism, reharmonization, or motivic development.
3. **Build to a peak** — highest register, densest harmony, or most dissonant point.
4. **Release** — resolve stepwise or by strong voice-leading back to the tonic, often landing on an extended chord (maj9, 6/9, add9) rather than a plain triad, so it feels warm rather than abrupt.

The "relief" only registers if there was real tension first. Don't skip the wandering part — that's what makes the landing feel earned.

## Harmonic tools that create tension you can release

- **Tritone substitutions** — replace a V7 with the dominant a tritone away for chromatic bass motion into the tonic.
- **Extended/altered dominants** (7b9, 7#9, 7#11, 7alt) right before resolving — the more altered the penultimate chord, the more satisfying the release to a clean tonic.
- **Pedal point** — hold the tonic or dominant in the bass while the upper voices move through unrelated harmony above it. Great for cadenzas because it keeps an anchor while everything else floats.
- **Chromatic planing / parallel harmony** — sliding the same chord shape chromatically creates a sense of drifting, which reads as "searching" emotionally.
- **Suspensions and appoggiaturas** — delay the chord tone by a step, then resolve down. This is probably the single most "emotional" device in tonal music — the delay creates longing, the resolution creates relief.
- **Borrowed chords (modal mixture)** — briefly darken a major cadenza with iv, bVI, or bVII before resolving to I major. The major landing feels brighter by contrast.

## Melodic/rhythmic devices

- **Rubato phrasing** — stretch and compress phrases freely; let some notes ring, rush through others. This is what makes it sound human rather than mechanical.
- **Sequence** — take a short motif and repeat it at different pitch levels, usually ascending, to build intensity before the peak.
- **Wide leaps followed by stepwise resolution** — a big interval creates instability; stepwise motion afterward feels like it's settling.
- **Space** — silence is one of your strongest tools. A pause right before the final resolution makes the listener lean in, which amplifies the release when it lands.
- **Quotation or motivic callback** — briefly reference the tune's main melodic hook inside the cadenza; it grounds the free playing and makes the ending feel connected rather than random.

## Voicing the final landing

How you land matters as much as how you got there:

- End on an extended chord (maj7, maj9, 6/9) instead of a bare triad — it's harmonically "unresolved" in a gentle way, which reads as warmth rather than finality.
- Voice the top note as the 9th or 6th rather than the root or 3rd — a non-root top note softens the ending.
- Let the bass arrive on the root a beat before the upper voices settle, or vice versa — staggering the resolution slightly avoids a "thud" landing.

## Practice approach

- **Transcribe real cadenzas** — Bill Evans (endings on "My Foolish Heart," "Peace Piece"), Art Tatum's stride cadenzas, Keith Jarrett's Köln Concert transitions, Oscar Peterson ballad endings, Coltrane's "Naima" intro/outro.
- **Practice guide-tone lines** (3rds and 7ths) through ii-V-I in every key so your ear knows exactly where tension wants to resolve — then you can delay that resolution on purpose.
- **Improvise cadenzas over a static tonic drone** — this forces you to create harmonic movement melodically, which is the core skill.
- **Record yourself and listen for the arc** — does it actually build somewhere, or does it just meander? The emotional effect comes from shape, not density of notes.

Here's a practical library of progressions for jazz cadenzas, organized by function — tension-builders, resolution devices, and coloristic ones for the "wandering" middle section.

## Resolution progressions (the "coming home" moments)

**ii–V–I (major)** — Cm7 – F7 – Bbmaj7
The bread-and-butter cadence of jazz. Smooth, logical, satisfying. Use it for the final resolution when you want clarity and closure rather than ambiguity. Found everywhere — Great American Songbook endings, bebop heads, ballad closes.

**ii–V–i (minor)** — Cm7b5 – F7alt – Bbm(maj7)
Darker, more introspective landing. Great when the tune itself is minor or modally ambiguous, or when you want the cadenza to end wistfully rather than triumphantly. Common in tunes like "Autumn Leaves," "Blue in Green," "Nardis."

**Tritone sub ii–V–I** — Cm7 – B7 – Bbmaj7
Same function as ii–V–I but the bass moves chromatically (F→B→Bb becomes smoother/more colorful via the tritone sub). Adds sophistication and a slight sense of surprise before landing. Very common in bebop and post-bop cadenzas — Bill Evans uses this constantly.

**Backdoor ii–V (bVII7 to I)** — Fm7 – Bb7 – Ebmaj7
Approaches the tonic "from behind" instead of the dominant. Feels unexpected but gentle — less "pulled" than a dominant resolution, more like drifting into home. Great for a soft, unresolved-feeling ending. Common in "There Is No Greater Love," modal jazz endings.

## Tension-building progressions (the "wandering" section)

**Coltrane changes (major thirds cycle)** — e.g., Bbmaj7 – D7 – Gmaj7 – Bb7 – Ebmaj7 – F#7 – Bmaj7
Moves through key centers a major third apart instead of the usual circle of fifths. Creates a spinning, kaleidoscopic feeling — disorienting in a beautiful way. Use this mid-cadenza when you want harmonic drama and unpredictability before settling down. Signature of Coltrane's "Giant Steps," also used by McCoy Tyner and later players as a cadenza device.

**Chromatic ii–V chains (descending)** — Dm7–G7 / Dbm7–Gb7 / Cm7–F7 / Bm7–E7...
Sequences the same ii–V shape down by half step. Builds intensity through repetition and chromatic descent — very "searching" quality. Great for the buildup phase right before the peak of a cadenza.

**Circle of fifths sequence** — Am7 – D7 – Gmaj7 – Cmaj7 – Fmaj7 – Bm7b5 – E7 – Am7
The most natural-sounding way to travel through multiple keys smoothly. Feels logical and inevitable rather than jarring, so it's good for a cadenza that wants to feel like a journey rather than a shock. Common in standards like "Autumn Leaves," "All The Things You Are."

**Parallel/planing dominant 7s (chromatic)** — F7 – E7 – Eb7 – D7 – Db7
Same chord quality sliding down chromatically with no functional pull. Creates an ambiguous, floating, almost impressionistic feeling — nothing resolves anything, it just drifts. Excellent for the most "free" part of a cadenza, right at the peak of tension before you decide to resolve. Evokes Wayne Shorter/McCoy Tyner modal color.

**Minor plagal motion (iv–i or iv–I)** — Ebm6 – Bb(maj7)
Borrowing the minor iv chord before landing on the major tonic. Very emotional, almost gospel/blues-inflected — a "sigh" quality. Great right at the very end for a warm, bittersweet landing. Used in ballad endings across the songbook (also very common in film scoring for the same reason).

**Suspended dominant chain (sus4 → 3rd resolution, delayed repeatedly)** — F7sus4 – F7sus4 – F7sus4 – Bbmaj9
Holding a sus chord and delaying its resolution repeatedly before finally letting the 3rd appear. Builds real anticipation — the ear wants that resolution and you keep withholding it. One of the most effective "relief" devices because the payoff is proportional to the wait. Common in modal jazz (Miles Davis, "So What" vocabulary) adapted into ballad cadenzas.

## Pedal-point progressions (anchor + floating harmony)

**Dominant pedal with shifting upper structures** — bass holds F while chords above move: F7 / Gb∆/F / F7#9 / F7alt / Bbmaj7
The bass note stays put (usually the dominant) while everything above it churns through tension. Feels grounded yet unstable at the same time — a good way to sustain tension for a long stretch without losing the listener. Extremely common in cadenzas specifically because it lets you improvise freely on top without losing the thread back to the tonic.

**Tonic pedal with borrowed chords above** — bass holds Bb while chords shift: Bb / Ebm/Bb / Bbmaj7 / Gb∆/Bb
Same idea but anchored on the tonic itself rather than the dominant — used more for the final "settling" part of a cadenza, where you want lush, unresolved-sounding color but the listener already senses you're home.

## Turnarounds reworked for slow/free cadenza use

**I–vi–ii–V slowed and reharmonized** — Bbmaj7 – Gm7 – Cm7 – F7(alt), stretched out with rubato and substitutions in between
The classic turnaround, but instead of playing it in time, you stretch each chord out and decorate it. Feels nostalgic and songlike — a good device when you want the cadenza to feel like it's "remembering" the tune's harmony one last time before ending.

**Backdoor turnaround** — Ebm7 – Ab7 – Dbmaj7 – (used as insert before final tonic)
Adds an unexpected detour on the way home. Good for a single surprising color chord right before your final ii-V-I, so the ending doesn't feel too predictable.

### How to sequence these into one cadenza

A strong template combining several of the above:

1. Start with a **dominant pedal** (float, low tension) as you leave the time feel.
2. Move into a **chromatic ii–V chain** or **Coltrane-style major-third cycle** to build real tension and cover harmonic distance.
3. Hit peak intensity with **parallel/planing dominants** — pure color, no resolution.
4. Pull back with a **suspended dominant chain**, delaying resolution to build anticipation.
5. Land using a **tritone-sub ii–V–I** or **backdoor ii–V**, voiced with an extended chord on top (9th or 6th) rather than the plain root.

## Form — the skeleton

- **AABA (32-bar)** — the classic songbook form ("I Got Rhythm," "Take the A Train"). The bridge (B section) is your chance to go somewhere harmonically different before returning home. Great for a strong, memorable hook.
- **ABAC / ABAB** — less symmetrical, more through-composed feel. Common in Ellington and later standards.
- **12-bar blues (and its variants)** — deceptively simple, but the best jazz blues heads (Monk, Mingus, Rollins) twist the form with substitutions, extra bars, or reharmonization while keeping the blues _feeling_.
- **Modal/open form** — fewer chord changes, longer stretches on one sonority (Miles Davis "So What," Coltrane "Impressions"). Puts weight on melody, rhythm, and improvisational development rather than harmonic motion.
- **Through-composed / suite-like** — no repeating section at all (a lot of Wayne Shorter, later Ellington/Strayhorn). Harder to pull off but can feel like a real narrative.

Pick the form based on what you want the piece to _do_: AABA if you want a hummable tune, blues if you want groove and blues language, modal/open if you want space for exploration.

## Melody — the part people actually remember

- **Write the melody before or independent of the chords when possible.** A strong melody can survive weak harmony; the reverse is rarely true.
- **Motivic development** — take a small rhythmic/melodic cell and vary it (invert it, extend it, sequence it) rather than writing 32 bars of unrelated phrases. This is what makes a tune feel _composed_ rather than noodled.
- **Contour and range** — think about the shape (arch, ascending, wave) and leave yourself somewhere to go; don't hit your highest note in bar 2.
- **Space** — rests are part of the melody. Some of the greatest heads (Monk especially) are as defined by silence as by notes.
- **Singability** — even instrumental heads benefit from being hummable. If a vocalist couldn't phrase it, reconsider.

## Harmony — beyond just "cool chords"

- **Write harmony that serves the melody**, not the other way around — figure out what chord _choices_ make a melody note feel surprising, inevitable, or poignant, rather than picking impressive chords and forcing a tune over them.
- **Contrast is more important than sophistication.** A plain diatonic A section makes a chromatic bridge hit way harder. Save your best reharmonization for the moment that needs it.
- **Study how the masters build tension across a whole form**, not just chord to chord — where does a Wayne Shorter tune first leave the key center? How long before it returns?
- **Decide on harmonic rhythm** — one chord per bar? Two? Static for four bars then fast motion? This affects the pacing/feel as much as the chords themselves.

## Rhythm and groove — often the most overlooked part

- **Decide the feel first**: swing, straight-eighths/Latin (bossa, samba, songo), waltz, odd meter (5/4, 7/4 — think "Take Five" or Brubeck generally), or a hybrid.
- **Syncopation in the melody** is often what separates a jazz head from a classical or pop tune — anticipating the beat, tying over the bar line, etc.
- **Rhythmic motifs**, not just melodic ones — a recurring rhythmic figure can unify a piece even when the pitches change.
- **Consider how it will feel to solo over** — is the form conducive to blowing (clear, singable changes) or is it more of a "concept piece" not meant for extended improvisation?

## Arranging and orchestration (if writing for a group)

- **Think about who states the melody and how** — unison horns? Harmonized? Call and response between instruments?
- **Countermelodies and background figures** — what happens under the melody the second time through, or behind a soloist?
- **Intro and outro** — these frame the piece and are a great place to use cadenza-like free material, vamps, or pedal points (like we discussed).
- **Dynamics and orchestral color** — even a small combo can build/release intensity through density, register, and who's playing, not just harmony.
- **Leave room for the band** — the best jazz compositions are frameworks for interaction, not fully fixed scores. Decide what's fixed (melody, key structural hits) and what's open (solo sections, vamps, choice of reharm).

## Title, concept, and identity

- A strong jazz piece often has a **concept or emotional throughline** — a story, a person, a place, a mood — that guides your choices (Ellington and Strayhorn were masters of this — "Chelsea Bridge," "Isfahan").
- **Know your influences but find your own harmonic/melodic fingerprint** — a favorite interval, a signature turnaround, a rhythmic tic. Great jazz composers are often recognizable within a few bars.

## Practical process

1. Start with a **motif or feel**, not a whole form — a 2-4 bar idea you actually like.
2. Sketch the **form on paper first** (even just letters: A-A-B-A, 8 bars each) before filling in details.
3. Write the **melody with minimal harmony first** — just enough to know the key centers — then go back and harmonize deliberately.
4. **Play it on an instrument, not just notation software** — feel matters, and jazz language lives in the body/ear as much as on the page.
5. **Get it in front of players** — jazz compositions often get refined through the rhythm section's interpretation. Real feedback from a live take reveals what's actually working.
6. Revise ruthlessly — cut anything that doesn't serve the melody, the groove, or the emotional arc.

// ═══ cadenza in bb — late-night jazz ballad, 72bpm ═══
// form: intro 4 | A 8 | B 8 | cadenza 5 | landing 3
setcpm(72 / 4) // one cycle = one bar of 4/4


const introChords = "<Bb^9 G-7 Eb^7 Bb^9>"
const aChords = "<Bb^7 G-7 C-7 F7sus Bb^7 G-7 [C-7 F7] Bb^7>"
const bChords = "<Eb^7 Eb-7 [D-7 G7] C-7 [C-7 B7] Bb^7 F7sus F7>"
const cadChords = "<F7sus Gb^7 F7b9 ~ [~ Bb^7 ~ ~]>" // piano lands a beat after the bass
const outChords = "<Bb^9 [~ Bb69 ~ ~] ~>" // 6/9 settles in a beat later

// swung comp rhythms: triplet grid, 3rd triplet = the 'a'
const rPush = "[x ~ ~] [~ x ~] [x ~ ~] [~ x ~]" // 1 · &2 · 3 · &4
const rStraight = "[x ~ ~] [x ~ ~] [~ x ~] [x ~ ~]" // 1 · 2 · &3 · 4
const rBusy = "[x ~ x] [~ x ~] [x ~ ~] [~ x ~]" // pushes for the B section
const rThaw = "[x ~ ~] [x ~ ~] [x ~ ~] [~ ~ ~]" // time dissolving into the cadenza
const rSparse = "[x ~ ~] [~ ~ ~] [x ~ ~] [~ ~ ~]" // final A bar breathes out

// piano comping
const comp = arrange(
  [4, chord(introChords)
    .anchor("bb4").voicing()
    .sound("piano").gain(.5).room(.9)],

  [8, chord(aChords)
    .struct(cat(rPush, rStraight, rPush, rStraight, rPush, rStraight, rPush, rSparse))
    .anchor("bb4").voicing()
    .sound("piano").gain(.55).room(.6).pan(.6)],

  [8, chord(bChords)
    .struct(cat(rBusy, rPush, rBusy, rStraight, rBusy, rPush, rBusy, rThaw))
    .anchor("bb4").voicing()
    .sound("piano").gain(.55).room(.6).pan(.6)],

  [5, chord(cadChords)
    .anchor("c5").voicing()
    .sound("piano").gain("<.5 .5 .62 .5 .55>").room(.8)],

  [3, chord(outChords)
    .anchor("c5").voicing()
    .sound("piano").gain(.5).room(.9)],
);

// acoustic bass — pedal · walking · pedal · root
const bass = arrange(
  [4, note("bb1")],
  [8, note(`<[bb1 d2 f2 a1] [g1 bb1 d2 db2] [c2 eb2 g2 f2] [f2 eb2 c2 a1] [bb1 d2 f2 a1] [g1 bb1 d2 b1] [c2 g1 f2 a1] [bb1 f2 d2 bb1]>`)],
  [8, note(`<[eb2 bb1 g1 d2] [eb2 bb1 gb1 eb2] [d2 a1 g1 b1] [c2 eb2 g2 b2] [c2 eb2 b1 ab1] [bb1 d2 f2 e2] [f2 eb2 c2 e2] [f2 eb2 c2 a1]>`)],
  [5, note("<f1 f1 f1 f1 bb1>")], // dominant pedal, then the root lands first
  [3, note("<bb1 bb1 ~>")],
).sound("gm_acoustic_bass").gain(.85).room(.15)

// tenor sax
const mel = arrange(
  [4, note("<~ ~ ~ [~ ~ ~ a4]>")], // lone pickup into A
  [8, note(`<[d5@3 c5] [bb4@2 g4 a4] [eb5@2 d5 c5] [c5@2 bb4 a4] [bb4@3 ~] [g4@2 bb4 d5] [eb5 d5 a4@2] [bb4@3 ~]>`)],
  [8, note(`<[g4@2 bb4 eb5] [eb5@2 d5 db5] [fs4 a4 b4@2] [eb5@3 c5] [d5 b4 ab4@2] [f5@2 eb5 d5] [c5@2 bb4 ab4] [a4@3 ~]>`)],
  [5, note(`<[f4 g4 bb4@2] [bb4 c5 d5@2] [d5 eb5 f5@2] [g5@2 [f5 eb5] d5] [~ ~ c5@2]>`)], // free line: sequence up, peak g5, silence, sax lands last on beat 3
  [3, note("<c5@4 [eb5@3 c5] ~>")], // 4th resolves down to the 9th
).sound("gm_tenor_sax").gain(.7).room(.5).delay(".18:.125:.25").pan(.45)

// strings pad under intro + landing
const pad = arrange(
  [4, chord(introChords).anchor("bb3").voicing().sound("gm_synth_strings_1").attack(.6).release(1.5).gain(.2).room(1)],
  [8, silence],
  [8, silence],
  [5, silence],
  [3, chord(outChords).anchor("bb3").voicing().sound("gm_synth_strings_1").attack(1.2).release(2).gain(.22).room(1)],
)

// drums — swing ride, feathered kick, hat chicks, backbeat + ghosts
const swingRide = "[rd ~ ~] [rd ~ rd] [rd ~ ~] [rd ~ rd]"
const hatChick = "[~ ~ ~] [hh ~ ~] [~ ~ ~] [hh ~ ~]"
const feather = "[bd ~ ~] [bd ~ ~] [bd ~ ~] [bd ~ ~]"
const snareBack = "[~ ~ ~] [sd ~ ~] [~ ~ ~] [sd ~ ~]"
const ghostA = "[~ ~ sd] [~ ~ ~] [~ ~ sd] [~ ~ ~]"
const ghostB = "[~ ~ ~] [~ ~ sd] [~ ~ ~] [~ ~ sd]"

const kit = stack(
  sound(swingRide).gain(.35).room(.35),
  sound(hatChick).gain(.3),
  sound(feather).gain(.15),
  sound(snareBack).gain(.38).room(.3),
  sound(cat(ghostA, ghostB)).gain(.15),
)

const drums = arrange(
  [4, silence],
  [8, stack(kit, sound("<cr ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~>").gain(.4).room(.5))],
  [8, stack(
    kit,
    sound("<cr ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~>").gain(.4).room(.5),
    sound("<~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ [[~ ~ ~] [sd ~ sd] [sd sd sd] [sd sd oh]]>").gain(.55).room(.4), // brush roll into the cadenza
  )],
  [5, sound("<~ ~ ~ ~ cr>").gain(.3).room(.6)], // time has stopped; one crash marks the landing
  [3, silence],
)

$: bass._pianoroll()
$: mel._pianoroll()
$: pad._pianoroll()
$: drums._pianoroll()
